## Question

Which of the following explains the most common effect that the process illustrated in the image had on relationships between states in Afro-Eurasia in the period 1450–1750 ?

## Answer Choices

- A. It led to deepening rivalries and conflicts as states’ military capabilities grew.
- B. It led to the emergence of new religious disputes over theological interpretations.
- C. It led to the establishment of pan-Eurasian nomadic empires, such as the Mongol Empire.
- D. It led to the adoption of feudal systems of government, as monarchs could not prevent the rising power of military aristocracies.

## Answer

> [!answer]- Reveal Answer
> **Correct Answer: A**
>
> ### Rationale
>
> Once a state's fiscal capacity allowed it to field artillery and disciplined musketeer infantry, it could defeat and absorb its neighbours — and its neighbours could do the same to it. Gunpowder therefore raised the stakes of interstate rivalry and shifted the balance toward large, well-funded agrarian empires. Between 1450 and 1750 this produced continuous war among the Ottomans, Safavids, Mughals, and European monarchies, and it is the most common pattern the process produced.
>
> **Why the other choices are wrong**
>
> - **B.** Rivalry over theology was not the most common effect, and the process shown produced no new religious disputes. If anything, rulers used established religion to legitimate their conquests. Sectarian conflict existed, but it was not what gunpowder states most typically generated between states.
> - **C.** The Mongol Empire was a product of the thirteenth century, formed by nomadic steppe peoples long before the gunpowder empires. Gunpowder states proved incapable of recreating pan-Eurasian nomadic empires — Darwin's argument in a later passage makes precisely this point.
> - **D.** Feudalism in western Europe emerged in the early medieval period, centuries before gunpowder. Monarchies that gained artillery revenue generally strengthened their own central authority over nobles rather than empowering military aristocracies.
